# My Takeaways from “How to Read a Paper”

The paper **“How to Read a Paper” by S. Keshav** explains a three-pass method for reading research papers. Instead of trying to understand everything from the first page itself, the method suggests reading the paper in stages and going into more detail with each pass. In the first pass, we mainly try to get an overall idea of the paper by looking at the title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusion, and references. We also try to understand things like what area the paper belongs to, what problem it is addressing, what the main contribution is, and whether the paper is clear and convincing. In the second pass, we focus more on the actual content, especially the important arguments, figures, graphs, and results, while not getting too stuck on complicated proofs. The third pass is the most detailed one, where we try to understand the paper deeply enough to almost recreate the work ourselves and also think critically about the assumptions made by the authors.

The part I found most useful was the idea that **not every research paper needs to be read in complete detail**. Since I am still new to research, I often feel that I need to understand every line, equation, and technical term before I can say that I have understood a paper. This method made me realize that this is not always necessary. The amount of time I spend on a paper should depend on how relevant it is to what I am working on. I particularly liked the first pass because it gives me a way to quickly understand the bigger picture before deciding whether the paper is actually worth spending more time on.

When I read research papers in the future, I want to try following these three passes instead of immediately starting from the first page and getting stuck on difficult concepts. I would first try to understand what problem the researchers are solving, what they have contributed, and why their work is important. If I find the paper useful for my topic, I can then go through the second and third passes to understand it in more depth. I think this approach could make research papers feel less intimidating and help me read them with a clearer purpose.
